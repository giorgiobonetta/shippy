The cycle

One cargo, end to end

Every decision moves both economics and probability, in opposite directions. That tension is the whole game.

  1. 1

    Origination

    A rolling market of cargoes, each with an origin, a destination, a tonnage and a capital requirement. Offers expire weekly, so waiting costs. Every corridor carries a congestion score that adds transit days and freight cost at the moment you book.

  2. 2

    Negotiation

    Price level, payment terms, pricing formula and delivery window. Selling on 30 days is a loan to your buyer: it wins the order and ties up your capital. A buyer's QP option earns a premium because you are short optionality you cannot hedge cleanly.

  3. 3

    Structuring

    Hedge ratio, FX cover, incoterm from EXW to DDP, insurance, inspection, carrier. Funding is a real choice: revolving facility, LC-backed trade finance, borrowing base against cargo and receivables, or your own balance sheet — each changing required equity, cost of funds and payment risk.

  4. 4

    Execution

    Documentary readiness, terminal slots, laytime and demurrage. Purchase and sales terms are explicit per route — FOB Port Hedland · index-linked selling CFR Shanghai · LC at sight — with the document set that trade actually needs: draft survey, Fe assay, moisture certificate, bill of lading, LC compliance.

  5. 5

    Settlement

    The result is broken out line by line: commercial margin, market exposure, FX, operations and claims, financing cost, storage optionality, credit result. The point the whole simulation is built around — commercial margin is not P&L.

What it teaches

Three things that are hard to see from outside

A hedge protects margin, not solvency

A cargo hedged at 100% has almost no flat-price exposure and can still take the desk under. The paper leg demands cash daily through variation margin; the physical leg pays once, at the end.

Price risk and liquidity risk are different risks, managed with different tools.

Working capital is the business

Once payment terms, transit days and quotational periods are modelled honestly, the days between paying your supplier and being paid by your buyer matter more to returns than the margin you negotiated. A shorter voyage on a thinner margin often wins.

Fixed costs decide who survives

While balancing the simulation I measured the opening position and found it structurally loss-making: roughly $38k of gross margin per cargo against $56k of overhead per cycle. No amount of good trading fixed it.

A desk's cost base sets the minimum flow it needs — a decision made before any trade is done.

Honest limits

What it deliberately leaves out

Being clear about the edges matters more than pretending there aren't any.

  • No order book, no market impact. Prices follow a mean-reverting process with seasonal volatility. You are a price taker.
  • Hedging is a ratio, not a strategy. No specific lots, no roll management, no options book. Basis risk is parameterised, not derived from two real curves.
  • One benchmark per commodity. No grade or brand differentials beyond a single hub basis.
  • Freight is a cost and an index, not a market. No voyage calculation, no bunker exposure, no contracts of affreightment.
  • Simplified credit. Counterparty limits and default probabilities are assumptions, not a rating model.
  • Storage optionality is a flag, not a real arbitrage against the curve.
